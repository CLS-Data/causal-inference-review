5.2 Matching

5.2.1 Method

Section 2.2.3 showed that confounding arises where treated and untreated individuals differ on background characteristics that also influence the outcome variable, with the consequence that a naïve comparison of outcomes would mix the effect of treatment with the effect of these pre-existing differences. Matching – a non-equivalent group design – addresses confounding by identifying sets of treated and untreated individuals who are similar on observed confounders. If these capture all the relevant differences between the groups, the only systematic difference within matched sets is treatment itself, and any difference in outcomes can be attributed to it. Like regression adjustment (Section 5.1), this relies on confounders being observed and measured well – matching on observed confounders does not ensure no systematic differences on unobserved confounders, too.

There are multiple matching algorithms, which differ in how ‘similar’ is defined. Many attempt to identify, for each treated individual, untreated individuals who are as similar as possible on confounders (or vice versa), with outcomes then compared within matched sets. Exact Matching identifies individuals who have the same values for a set of characteristics. However, as the number of possible combinations of these characteristics explodes exponentially as more are added (the curse of dimensionality), exact matching can only be feasibly done for a small number of characteristics with few unique values or, alternatively, with sample sizes that are very large (e.g., as in whole population administrative data). Coarsened Exact Matching makes matching more feasible by coarsening the variables used to match upon – e.g., matching within age groups, rather than by integer age. But again, the curse of dimensionality applies – with just four variables, each taking four values, there are 256 (44) possible strata in the data. Matching can instead be approximate and utilise statistical measures of ‘distance’ that aggregate difference on many dimensions simultaneously. For instance, individuals may be matched upon Mahalanobis distance, a multivariate analogue of Cohen’s d. Though matching may not be exact, differences between treatment and control are made smaller than they otherwise would be.

Another alternative metric is the propensity score (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983). In propensity score matching (PSM), individuals in treatment and comparison groups are matched upon their probability of having been treated, with this probability typically estimated (e.g., using a regression model) based on a set of predictors of treatment. Importantly, this probability is a single scalar value (ranging 0-1) making matching more straightforward. However, as there may be numerous profiles of covariates that yield the same probability of being treated, pairs of individuals matched upon propensity scores do not necessarily share similar values on underlying covariates – balance is instead a property of the overall matched sample rather than of each matched pair. Yet, given covariates can differ within matched pairs, where poor matches are pruned past the point groups are approximately balanced, matching upon propensity scores can increase the risk of imbalance (King & Nielsen, 2019), though the extent to which this matters in practice has been questioned (Ripollone et al., 2018). Matching on the propensity score can also reduce statistical efficiency compared to more direct matching methods. (Note, the issue of imbalance applies to using the propensity score to identify matches, rather than for inverse probability weighting, which is discussed in Section 5.3.)

Matching does not have to be 1:1 – several matches can be obtained for each ‘treated’ individual and vice versa. Matches can also be made with replacement, so that one individual may be matched to multiple others. The advantage of matching with replacement is that it can reduce bias as the best match is used in each case. However, it also decreases statistical power as the effective sample size is reduced – in other words, there is a bias-variance trade-off (Huntington-Klein, 2022). Conversely, using several matches per individual increases statistical power but also increases bias as more data than just the very best match are used.1 Bias can be reduced by using a caliper – i.e., setting a maximum dissimilarity between matches – and by using kernel weighting, where matches are weighted more highly the better they are. An alternative is entropy balancing (Hainmueller, 2012), which instead of identifying similar individuals identifies weights to create matched treatment and control groups that are overall balanced for specified moments of the distributions of the matching variables (e.g., same means, variance and skewness for covariates).

As with statistical adjustment, the validity of causal estimates obtained via matching depends on whether, within matched groups, there is no remaining confounding. Unlike statistical adjustment, matching can be thought of as a data preprocessing step rather than an estimation procedure, per se (Ho et al., 2007) – matching is a way to identify an appropriate analytical sample before substantive analyses are undertaken. Matching can therefore be combined with regression – e.g., by estimating a regression model within a group of matched individuals. This two-step approach has the advantage that it can be “doubly robust” (Stuart, 2010) – robust to the failures of one method, if the other is valid. For instance, a valid causal estimate may be obtained with a mis-specified regression model, if matching was sufficient for achieving non-confoundedness, or vice versa. Note, where treated individuals are matched to, matching yields the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT). This may not be equal to the ATE if there is effect heterogeneity.2

Matching has several advantages versus regression adjustment. First, matching itself does not require the outcome to be modelled as the method focuses on approximating the data-generating process for treatment specifically. This is useful where it is difficult to model the outcome (e.g., where the outcome is rare) or where there are multiple outcome variables – i.e., when performing an outcome-wide epidemiological analysis (VanderWeele, 2017; VanderWeele et al., 2020). One drawback, however, is Z-bias (Section 5.1) if unobserved confounders remain.

A second advantage of matching is that it can ensure comparisons between treatment and control groups are made within regions of ‘common support’ (overlap in covariate distributions), whereas regression approaches, which interpolate between data points, do not. Some combinations of characteristics may not appear in both treated and control groups (i.e., positivity may be violated) – this is at least explicit in matching but hidden when performing regression. While interpolation may be reasonable in a given case, its validity cannot be ascertained with the data at hand. Interpolation is also more plausible if matching has been used as a data preprocessing step. Relatedly, the assumptions of propensity score matching are partly testable as balance can be assessed within strata of the propensity score, though of course this does not tell the analyst anything about confounders that are unobserved.

Matching does have several disadvantages, however. First, if matches cannot be obtained for all observations, some units may need to be discarded, potentially yielding a valid causal estimate, but within a sample with no clear interpretation or potentially no wider applicability. Second, matching creates challenges where exposures are continuous rather than binary, though extensions, such as kernel matching, have been developed.

5.2.2 Applications

Persson (2014) uses data from BCS70 to assess whether the strong association between higher education and political behaviour (e.g., voting in general elections) reported in prior studies is causal. Unlike data used in previous studies, the BCS70 allowed the author to match upon important confounders measured prospectively from childhood, such as parental behaviour and cognitive ability at ages 5 and 10 years. The author used genetic matching – an algorithm that identifies weights for a generalised Mahalanobis distance matrix minimising imbalance between treatment and control groups (Diamond & Sekhon, 2013) – observing that the procedure performs better than coarsened exact matching and propensity score matching in the BCS70 sample (due to larger retained samples and better balance, respectively). He finds little evidence of association after matching is performed. In support of the matching, the author reports multiple tests of balance, including a comparison of the imbalance remaining against that expected under random assignment and placebo tests using pre-treatment variables that higher education could not have caused. A caveat is that only 569 cohort members were treated and confidence intervals are not reported – the analysis indicates no detectable effect rather than the absence of one.

In two papers, Jerrim & Sims (2019, 2020) use data from the MCS to assess the causal effect of grammar (i.e., selective) school attendance upon an array of non-cognitive factors, such as wellbeing and educational aspirations. As with Persson (2014), the authors exploit the rich life course information on confounding variables (e.g., prior measures of cognitive ability and wellbeing), though do not observe entry test results, the main basis upon which entry is determined. In both papers, the authors match upon propensity scores, but use two separate groups from which to draw untreated observations. In Jerrim & Sims (2019), the authors use geographic variation across Local Education Authority (LEA) areas, comparing those attending grammar schools with matched cohort members living in LEAs with no selective schools at all. In Jerrim & Sims (2020), the authors compare individuals who attended grammar school with those who applied but ultimately did not attend.3 They find little evidence of association with non-cognitive outcomes overall, except some evidence of an effect on educational aspirations (Jerrim & Sims, 2019). One limitation of their papers is a lack of discussion of what unobserved confounders may be present, especially for the analysis using grammar school applicants for the control group. A second is precision: the 2019 estimates draw on 4,785 cohort members, whereas the 2020 analysis matches roughly 650 in England and 500 in Northern Ireland. Several estimates are large enough to be substantively meaningful but have wide confidence intervals.

5.2.3 Opportunities

As with statistical adjustment (Section 5.1), there are ample opportunities in CLS’s cohorts to apply matching methods. An additional advantageous property of CLS’s cohorts is their large sample size – matching can lead to a large loss of observations if few or no comparison observations can be found for a treated observation. Though not a design that matches on treatment, CLS’s cohorts can also provide general population controls in case-control designs – for instance, the MCS provided a control group as part of the National Evaluation of Sure Start (Joshi & Fitzsimons, 2016) and the NCDS is part of the Wellcome Case Control Consortium, which was used in many of the first molecular genetic studies of health and disease (Wellcome Trust Case Control Consortium, 2007).
